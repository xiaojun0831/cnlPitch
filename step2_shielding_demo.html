Research behind the model
  • Onset beam is real but short and field-aligned. GLE onsets are rapid, anisotropic beams that follow the interplanetary magnetic field (IMF), then isotropize; the beam persists only for well-connected events and weakens with connection angle (Bruno & Dalla 2026, A&A 708). Anisotropy is typically high within the first hour (GLE 72 studies).
  • Aim the shield along the IMF, not at the Sun. Parker-spiral angle is about 45° at 1 AU, about 52° mid-transit (1.25 AU) and about 57° at Mars for 400 km/s wind (my calculation), so a Sun-facing shield misses the beam. The pointing-error slider models this.
  • The early window is quiet because flux is low, and the danger comes later. Early intensities sit near a streaming limit; the peak arrives at shock passage, 10–100× higher, and in the Aug 1972 event that was the real threat (Reames, NASA NTRS 2000). Large events peaked 14–69 h after onset (UNH EMMREM table: 1960, 1972, 1989, 2000) and last hours to days.
  • Hard events break the shortcut. High-energy protons peak early, so the model raises the early plateau with energy: a GLE-like event is worst in the first hours.
  • Waiting is not free. The scan adds the dose the crew collects while sheltered from now until the EVA starts (shelter shielding slider, behind the tank on NTP) to the EVA dose. A late window only counts as open if the whole wait plus the EVA fits in the budget.
  • Ship type. NTP: LH₂ tank shadow (shielding depends on tank fill versus proton range) plus a reactor residual-dose term at the worksite. NEP: no bulk LH₂ tank, so no tank shadow and the shelter-shielding slider carries the whole SPE defense; the reactor term defaults lower. Reactor term is a placeholder: exponential decay (e-fold 48 h), same dose to every organ, not reduced by the tank shadow.
  • HZETRN2020 (not re-implemented here). It transports protons and secondary neutrons/pions in 3D through ray-traced shielding (vehicle, suit, tank) onto a phantom body, so organ dose depends on orientation in anisotropic fields (Wilson & Nealy 1993). Skin and lens get the most SPE dose, deep organs the least. Here that is reduced to the “Other” inputs plus exponential organ-depth factors.
  • Decay assumption. Exponential, τ = 12 h at 50 MeV, shortening for harder events. Literature: the ≥100 MeV channel decayed with an e-fold of about 3.4 h (Dec 2006); decay often has two phases, 10–20 h then over 40 h in the reservoir; low-energy decay is exponential in about 90% of events, less often above 30–60 MeV. The model has no slow reservoir tail, so late-time dose is underestimated.
  • One energy stands in for a spectrum. Real SPE spectra are continuous and the high-energy tail leaks through shielding, so tank benefits are overstated here. The tank transmission curve exp(−3·r1.5), where r is tank depth ÷ proton range, is a smooth placeholder and bottoms out at the secondary-radiation floor once r is above about 1.5.
  • Placeholders, not calibrated. The flux regression and occurrence rate (2.65, 1.85, 7.5, −1.35) came from the Gemini code and I could not trace them to a published catalog. The dose coefficient is now an input. I lowered the default from 3 to 0.6: at 3 the design event gave about 5.8 Gy-Eq to blood-forming organs in open space, which is far too high against my recollection of historical large events, and every EVA was a permanent no-go. At 0.6 it is about 1.2 Gy-Eq, which is probably still high, but it is low enough that the default case shows the differences between the ships. At 0.3 an NTP crew can go at any time with the default settings, so the result depends heavily on this one number. Replace it with HZETRN response functions and a real SPE catalog before using any number in the case.
